# The Deal Gap

### How to Know If a Rental Is Worth Buying Before You Make an Offer

*A short guide for new residential real estate investors*

---

**Disclaimer:** This book is educational. It is not personalized financial, legal, tax, or investment advice. Real estate involves risk. Always verify numbers with your own due diligence and licensed professionals before you buy.

## Why This Book Exists

Most first deals don’t fail because new investors are lazy.

They fail because someone paid too much — or bought the wrong strategy for the house they fell in love with.

Listing sites are built to sell you a story: staged photos, sunny descriptions, a Zestimate that feels official. None of that answers the only question that matters to an investor:

> **How far is the asking price from the price where this deal actually works for me?**

That distance is the **Deal Gap**.

This book teaches you how to find it, read it, and close it — or walk away with confidence.

**How to use this book**

1. Read it once for the framework (Income Value → Target Buy → Deal Gap).
2. Keep the cheat sheet when you analyze addresses.
3. Run the routine on real listings until the three numbers feel automatic.

**By the end, you should be able to look at any listing and answer:**  
*Is there a Deal Gap — and can I close it?*

# Part I — Why Most New Investors Get Stuck

## Chapter 1: The Listing Trap

Real estate sites market properties. They do not tell you if the deal works.

That is not a conspiracy. It is the business model. Photos, map pins, and “days on market” help a buyer *browse*. Investors need a *decision*.

You can spend an entire Sunday evening scrolling “newest listings,” hearting open houses, and arguing with a partner about granite versus quartz — and still not know whether a single address produces cash flow at a price you can defend. That is the listing trap: activity that feels like investing but is actually window shopping with higher stakes.

### Two lenses

| Marketing lens | Investor lens |
|---|---|
| “Do I love this kitchen?” | “Does this cash flow for *my* goals?” |
| “Is the neighborhood trendy?” | “What rent can I defend?” |
| “Is the Zestimate high?” | “What is *my* Target Buy?” |

The marketing lens is emotional and fast. The investor lens is boring — and it saves you from a five-figure mistake.

Neither lens is evil. You will live with (or manage) the kitchen. You should also refuse to let the kitchen set the purchase price.

### The expensive first-mistake problem

One bad deal can cost more than years of tools, courses, and coaching combined. Not because you “missed appreciation,” but because you locked in a payment the rent cannot support.

The pain is rarely dramatic on closing day. It shows up as:

- Rent that covers “most” of the payment — until vacancy  
- A special assessment or insurance renewal that erases your year  
- A refinance that never comes because the appraisal did not care about your Instagram rehab

New investors often compensate by collecting more tabs: Zillow, Redfin, Rentometer, a spreadsheet someone shared in a Facebook group. More data without a decision system just creates fog.

### The reframe

Stop scrolling listings hoping a deal reveals itself.

Start hunting with a number: your **Target Buy**. Everything else — offers, strategy, negotiation — hangs off that number.

Listing sites end at price. Investor calculators often end at “cash flow is negative.” This book — and the Deal Gap method — exist for the step after both: *how far am I, and what do I do next?*

**Try it on a real address:** Open one listing you almost liked last month. Write one sentence from the marketing lens and one from the investor lens. Notice which sentence your brain prefers — and which one protects your money.

## Chapter 2: What “A Good Deal” Actually Means

A good deal is not:

- “Cheap”
- “Hot market”
- “Zestimate looks high”
- “My uncle said rents are crazy here”
- “It won’t last a weekend on the market”

A good deal means:

> The asking price is close enough to **your Target Buy** that you can win the property with price, capital, income truth, or structure — without abandoning your return goals.

Sometimes that means a small Deal Gap and a straightforward offer.  
Sometimes that means a large Deal Gap and creative financing.  
Sometimes that means **pass**.

Passing is not failure. Passing is the skill that keeps you solvent long enough to find the deal that fits.

### Good deal ≠ good house

A renovated craftsman can be a terrible LTR at asking. A tired duplex can be an excellent house hack. “Good” is relative to strategy, capital, and Target Buy — not relative to how you feel walking through the front door.

Investors who skip this distinction end up stretching rent assumptions to justify emotional purchases. That stretch has a name later: phantom deal.

### The core question

Write this on a sticky note:

> **How far is the asking price from the price where this deal actually works for me?**

That distance — as a percent of asking — is the Deal Gap. The rest of this book is how to compute the pieces honestly and act on the result.

Notice the words **for me**. Two investors can share a listing and get different Deal Gaps because their financing, buy discount, and strategy differ. That is a feature. Your number is personal on purpose.

**Try it on a real address:** Before you calculate anything, guess the Deal Gap on one listing (0–5%, 5–15%, or 15%+). Keep the guess. You will check it after Chapter 6.

# Part II — The Deal Gap Framework

This is the teachable core. If you only remember one section, remember this one.

## Chapter 3: The Three Numbers That Matter

Hours of analysis compress into three numbers.

Most beginner content teaches twenty metrics at once: cap rate, CoC, DSCR, IRR, GRM, equity multiple. Those metrics matter later. On day one they create fog. You do not need twenty dials to avoid a bad first purchase. You need a ceiling, a personal buy number, and the distance between that number and the seller’s ask.

### 1. Income Value

**Income Value** is the ceiling price before the deal stops making sense as an income property — the cash-flow boundary under your assumptions.

Think of it as: *If I pay more than this, the property’s income cannot support the deal the way I need it to.*

Income Value is not “what the house is worth on Zillow.” Market value is what a buyer might pay. Income Value is what *this income* can support *under your loan and expense assumptions*. Those can diverge wildly in hot neighborhoods with mediocre rents.

You do not need a finance degree. You need defendable rent, honest expenses, and a clear financing picture. Chapter 4 builds this.

### 2. Target Buy

**Target Buy** is the price that hits *your* return target — usually Income Value reduced by a buy discount (a margin for error, time, and required return).

Default teaching shorthand many investors start with:

```
Target Buy ≈ Income Value × (1 − buy discount %)
```

Example: Income Value $380,000 × (1 − 5%) = **$361,000 Target Buy**.

Why not just buy at Income Value? Because breakeven is fragile. One vacant month, one roof repair, one insurance renewal — and your “fine” deal is a second job you did not apply for. Target Buy builds in the margin that lets you sleep.

Target Buy is your offer anchor and your walk-away number. Chapter 5 makes it personal.

### 3. Deal Gap

**Deal Gap** is the percentage distance between asking (or the relevant market ask) and Target Buy:

```
Deal Gap % = (Asking Price − Target Buy) / Asking Price × 100
```

| Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Positive** | Asking is above Target Buy — you need that % concession *or* another path to close the gap |
| **Near zero / negative** | Rare — the deal may work at or above asking; look closer |

Important: a positive Deal Gap is not a moral judgment of the seller. Sellers ask what they hope to get. You bid what you can defend. The gap is the negotiation — and the creativity — in between.

### Micro-example

- Asking price: **$400,000**
- Target Buy: **$360,000**
- Deal Gap: `(400,000 − 360,000) / 400,000 × 100` = **10%**

A 10% Deal Gap does not mean “bad house.” It means: *At asking, this is not yet your deal.* Your job is to close that gap — or choose a different strategy — or pass.

### Why percent, not just dollars?

Dollars matter in the offer. Percent helps you compare across price points. A $40,000 gap on a $200,000 house (20%) is a different fight than a $40,000 gap on a $800,000 house (5%). Bands in Chapter 6 use percent so you can build intuition quickly.

### Visual (the stack)

```
Asking price          ████████████████████  $400,000
Income Value          ███████████████████   $380,000
Target Buy            ██████████████████    $360,000

Deal Gap = distance from Ask → Target Buy = 10%
```

Income Value sits above Target Buy when you require a discount. Asking often sits above both. The Deal Gap is the stretch you must close.

### What these three numbers replace

| Old habit | Three-number replacement |
|---|---|
| “Looks like a good area” | Income Value from defendable rent |
| “I might get it for a little less” | Target Buy written before the tour |
| “We’ll see how negotiation goes” | Deal Gap band → pursue / structure / pass |

**Try it on a real address (exercise):**  
Asking $350,000. Target Buy $315,000. What is the Deal Gap?  
*(Answer: 10%.)*

## Chapter 4: How to Build Income Value (Without the Fog)

Income Value collapses if the inputs are fantasy. This chapter is about honesty.

If you only skim one chapter in Part II, skim Chapter 3 for the definitions — then slow down here. Bad Income Value is how smart people buy dumb deals with beautiful spreadsheets.

### Start with rent you can defend

“It’ll rent for…” is how phantom deals are born.

Defendable rent means you can point to comparable leased properties, conservative sources, or a blended estimate — not a hopeful screenshot from the nicest listing on the block.

Ask:

- What have similar homes **actually leased for** in the last few months?
- Is the listing’s rent estimate the renovated version of a house that is not renovated yet?
- Are you accidentally comps-shopping the best street in the ZIP?

If sources disagree, do not average in a number you cannot explain. Pressure-test a **range** (low / base / high) and see how Income Value moves. If the deal only works at the high rent, you do not have a deal — you have a hope.

### Operating reality (why the “1% rule” fails)

Gross rent is not profit. At minimum, account for:

- Vacancy
- Property taxes
- Insurance
- Maintenance / repairs
- Property management (even if you self-manage — your time is not free)
- Other fixed costs (HOA, landscaping, utilities you pay)

Shortcuts like “rent should be 1% of price” skip local taxes, insurance shock markets, and financing. They feel decisive. They are often wrong.

A house that “should” rent for $4,000 on a $400,000 price tag but actually rents for $2,400 is not a 1% marvel. It is a cautionary tale with granite counters.

### A simple monthly shape (teaching)

You do not need perfect accounting to think clearly:

```
Gross rent
− Vacancy allowance
= Effective income
− Operating expenses (tax, insurance, maint, PM, other)
= Money available for loan payment + your cash flow goal
```

Income Value is the purchase price at which that shape still works given your loan. When expenses rise (insurance in coastal or catastrophe-exposed markets, taxes after reassessment), Income Value falls — even if the kitchen is perfect.

### Financing changes the ceiling

Down payment, interest rate, and loan term change what purchase price the income can support. Two investors can share the same rent and expenses and get different Income Values because their capital stacks differ.

Examples of drift:

- You modeled 25% down, then switched to 5% down “so I can buy sooner” — payment jumps, Income Value drops, Deal Gap widens.
- You modeled 6.5%, then locked 7.25% — same story.
- You forgot closing costs and upfront repairs — your cash-in changed even if price did not.

Write your financing assumptions down before you tour. Changing them mid-crush is how Target Buy quietly drifts upward.

### Never fabricate missing inputs

If insurance is unknown, mark **Unavailable** — then get a quote. If rent is unknown, mark a range and widen your required discount until you have better data.

Fabricated inputs create confident wrong answers. Honest gaps create better questions.

Professional-looking models fail the same way amateur ones do: someone typed a guess in a cell and forgot it was a guess.

### Soft note on multi-source estimates

A single Zestimate is a clue, not a verdict. Blending multiple in-range sources (when available) beats trusting one logo. When a source is missing, leave it missing.

This is also why “the app said so” is not a personality. Whether you use a spreadsheet or a decision engine, you should be able to point to the rent and expense assumptions underneath Income Value.

**Try it on a real address:** Pick one listing. Write rent, taxes, insurance, vacancy, and management as either a number you can defend or “unknown.” Count your unknowns. That count is your humility score.

## Chapter 5: From Income Value to Target Buy

Breakeven is not a win.

Income Value tells you where the deal stops making sense as income. Target Buy tells you where the deal is *worth doing for you*.

### Why you need margin

You need room for:

- Vacancy lasting longer than the model
- Repairs you did not see on the tour
- Rate or insurance changes
- The return you actually require for tying up capital and time

That margin is your **buy discount** (or required return buffer). It is a personal setting, not a market slogan.

Think of Income Value as the guardrail on a mountain road. Target Buy is the lane you choose inside that guardrail. Driving on the guardrail is technically “still on the road.” It is also how you meet the canyon.

### Make the discount yours

Beginners often start around a modest discount (for teaching, 5% off Income Value is a clean default). Aggressive investors may require more. House hackers sometimes accept less cash-flow margin because housing offset is part of the return.

| Investor situation | Discount posture (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| First rental, thin reserves | Wider discount — more margin |
| Experienced operator, strong reserves | May accept tighter margin |
| House hack with housing offset | Cash-flow margin can be smaller *if* the offset is real |
| Heavy rehab / uncertain rent | Wider discount until bids and leases firm up |

The point is not the exact default. The point is that **you chose it on purpose** before emotion entered the chat.

### Target Buy = offer anchor + walk-away

- **Anchor:** Your first serious offer math starts here (then you choose path: price, capital, income, structure).
- **Walk-away:** If you cannot close the Deal Gap without violating Target Buy, you leave.

If Target Buy floats every time you like a kitchen, you do not have a Target Buy. You have a mood.

### “But someone else will pay more”

Yes. Someone else might. That someone else might also have different capital, a different strategy, or a worse spreadsheet. Your job is not to win every house. Your job is to win houses that fit *your* Target Buy.

Paying someone else’s number to satisfy your ego is how Deal Gap analysis dies in the driveway after the inspection.

### Exercise — one page before the next tour

Write:

1. Strategy (e.g., long-term rental)
2. Defendable monthly rent
3. Monthly operating costs (or annual ÷ 12)
4. Financing: down payment %, rate, term
5. Buy discount %
6. Resulting Income Value and Target Buy (even if rough)

Tape it inside your laptop lid.

**Try it on a real address:** Complete the one-pager for a property you will not buy. Practice without attachment.

## Chapter 6: Reading the Deal Gap Like a Pro

Deal Gap is a **decision signal**, not destiny.

New investors often want the gap to spit out “buy” or “don’t buy.” Reality is messier — and more useful. The gap tells you how much work stands between the seller’s wish and your constraint. Then you decide whether that work is worth it.

### Plain bands (not false precision)

Use bands to decide *effort*, not to pretend the third decimal is magic:

| Deal Gap | Typical read for a new investor |
|---|---|
| **0–5%** | Close enough that a normal negotiation or small structure tweak may work |
| **5–15%** | Real work — price, capital, income re-check, or creative structure |
| **15%+** | Hard mode — need a clear path, a different strategy, or a pass |

Seller motivation, days on market, condition, and your local competition all matter. The band tells you how hard the math fight will be — not whether the house is “good.”

A 3% gap on a bidding-war listing may still lose to cash. A 16% gap on a stale listing with a relocating seller may close with structure. Bands are a starting playbook, not a court verdict.

### Big gaps can still close

A 18% Deal Gap on a tired listing with a motivated seller and seller financing might be more workable than a 6% gap on a bidding-war reno with no flexibility.

The price tag is not the deal. **The structure is.**

When you feel yourself saying “there’s no way,” translate it: “There’s no way *on price alone with my current capital.*” That sentence leaves room for Paths 2–4 in Chapter 9.

### Common misreads

1. **Confusing market value with Target Buy** — What it might appraise for ≠ what works for your cash flow.
2. **Confusing cash-flow deals with appreciation bets** — If your thesis is “it will go up,” say that out loud. Do not hide an appreciation bet inside fake rent.
3. **Treating Deal Gap as a moral score** — A large gap is information. It is not a personal insult from the seller.
4. **Ignoring strategy** — A “bad” LTR gap can be a “fine” house-hack gap (see Appendix Example B).
5. **Precision theater** — Arguing about 11.2% vs 11.4% while rent is ±$200/mo is rearranging deck chairs.

### Checklist: pursue / pass / change strategy

**Pursue** when you can name at least one credible path to close the gap without lying to your spreadsheet.

**Pass** when closing the gap requires fantasy rent, zero repairs, or a discount the seller has no reason to give — and no alternate structure exists.

**Change strategy** when the house fails as a long-term rental but might work as a house hack, wholesale, BRRRR, or flip (Part III).

### A 60-second self-test

1. Say your Target Buy out loud.  
2. Say the Deal Gap band.  
3. Say your first path — or “pass.”  

If you stumble, your inputs are still fog. Clarity is the product; speed is optional.

# Part III — Same Property, Different Strategies

## Chapter 7: One Address, Six Paths

Strategy choice changes Target Buy and Deal Gap. Same roof, different math.

If you only ever underwrite “buy and hold LTR,” you will pass on houses that fit you — and chase houses that only fit someone else’s strategy. Rank first. Crush later.

### Long-Term Rental (LTR)

- **Fits:** Investors who want durable cash flow and simpler operations
- **“Good” looks like:** Defendable rent, manageable expenses, Target Buy within reach
- **Main risk:** Overpaying relative to rent; underwriting vacancy and repairs poorly
- **Deal Gap shift:** Most “default” analyses live here — your baseline gap

LTR is the baseline for this book because the Income Value story is easiest to see. It is not the only path to a first win.

### Short-Term Rental (STR)

- **Fits:** Operators comfortable with hospitality, local rules, and variable occupancy
- **“Good” looks like:** ADR × occupancy that survives regulations and seasonality
- **Main risk:** Ban risk, cleanup costs, optimistic occupancy
- **Deal Gap shift:** Higher income can shrink the gap — if the income is real

Rule of thumb: if the deal only works at peak-season occupancy forever, it does not work.

### BRRRR (Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat)

- **Fits:** Investors who can manage rehab and refinance risk
- **“Good” looks like:** ARV and after-repair rent support refinance and cash recovery goals
- **Main risk:** Rehab overruns; refinance appraisal miss
- **Deal Gap shift:** Entry price + rehab must still pencil to Target Buy on the *stabilized* asset

BRRRR fails when people underwrite the exit refinance as a certainty and the rehab as a Pinterest board.

### Fix & Flip

- **Fits:** Project managers who profit from forced equity, not monthly rent
- **“Good” looks like:** Clear ARV, tight budget, timeline that survives holding costs
- **Main risk:** Over-rehab; soft exit market
- **Deal Gap shift:** “Target Buy” is really max allowable offer — gap is vs asking on a wholesale/retail buy

### House Hack

- **Fits:** Owner-occupants offsetting housing cost (house, duplex, small multi)
- **“Good” looks like:** Housing cost offset + loan program fit (e.g., low-down owner-occ)
- **Main risk:** Living with tenants / roommate reality; exit if life changes
- **Deal Gap shift:** Personal housing savings can justify a Target Buy an LTR investor would reject

See Appendix Example B — same ask, much smaller gap when housing offset is real.

### Wholesale

- **Fits:** Deal finders who assign contracts rather than hold
- **“Good” looks like:** Spread between your contract price and a buyer’s Target Buy
- **Main risk:** No end buyer; thin margins; compliance
- **Deal Gap shift:** You care about the *buyer’s* gap — your fee lives in between

**Try it on a real address:** For one listing, write “works / maybe / no” for LTR and one other strategy. Do not calculate yet — gut rank first, then check with numbers.

## Chapter 8: Pick the Strategy Before You Fall in Love With the House

Falling in love first is how LTR assumptions get stretched to justify a house-hack crush (or vice versa).

### Match strategy to constraints

Ask before you tour:

- **Capital:** How much cash can I deploy?
- **Time:** Can I manage rehab or STR ops?
- **Skills:** Am I an operator or a capital allocator?
- **Rules:** HOA, STR bans, lending overlays, insurance availability

### Rank before you offer

Score the same property across strategies *before* you write an offer. You are not looking for a story. You are looking for the least-bad path to a Target Buy you can defend.

### Case sketch (same house, different verdict)

Imagine a 3/2 near a college:

- As **LTR** at asking: 14% Deal Gap — rent does not support the payment.
- As **house hack** (owner occupies, rents rooms): housing offset shrinks the effective gap — pursue.
- As **wholesale**: if a BRRRR buyer’s Target Buy sits under asking by enough to leave assignment fee room — maybe.
- As **STR**: city just restricted stays under 30 days — pass.

The house did not change. The strategy did.

**Try it on a real address:** Name your capital, time, and skill constraints in one line each. Circle the *one* strategy that fits those lines this year.

# Part IV — Closing the Gap

## Chapter 9: The Four Paths to Close a Deal Gap

When Deal Gap is positive, you are not stuck with “ask for a miracle price cut.” You have levers.

Most beginners only practice Path 1. That is why they think every 12% gap is impossible. Pros ask which lever moves with the least fantasy.

### Path 1 — Price

Negotiate toward Target Buy. Cleanest path when the seller has room and motivation.

Works best when: days on market are high, condition issues are documented, or the seller’s next plan (job move, purchase contingency) needs certainty more than a trophy price.

### Path 2 — Capital

Change down payment, bring partners, or adjust reserves so the financing picture supports a higher workable price — *without* pretending expenses went away.

Caution: “I’ll just put less down” often *widens* the gap because the payment grows. Capital path means improving the stack intelligently (partner equity, different product, reserves that allow a tighter discount) — not magical leverage.

### Path 3 — Income re-verify

Better rent comps, true taxes/insurance, corrected square footage, or a value source you missed. Sometimes the gap shrinks because the *inputs* were wrong — not because the seller moved.

This is the path ego skips. Re-verification can also *widen* the gap when insurance quotes come in hot. That is still a win: you avoided a phantom deal.

### Path 4 — Structure / creative finance

Seller carry, subject-to, blended financing, rate help, credits — tools that change cash flow without requiring the full retail price cut. Learn the vocabulary; run the numbers; stay compliant. (See the glossary and [dealgapiq.com/glossary](https://dealgapiq.com/glossary) for plain-English definitions.)

Structure is not a party trick. It is how two parties meet when retail cash-to-seller and retail cash-from-buyer cannot.

> **Theme line:** The price tag isn’t the deal. The structure is.

Often the winning offer is a **blend**: a smaller price ask + a capital tweak + a verification that lifts rent confidence.

### Choosing a first path (practical)

| Situation | First path to try |
|---|---|
| Stale listing, clear repair list | Price (with evidence) |
| Thin comps / wild rent estimates | Income re-verify |
| Seller needs monthly income more than top dollar | Structure |
| You are under-reserved for the strategy | Capital (or pass) |
| 15%+ gap, no motivation signals | Pass or change strategy |

**Try it on a real address:** Assume a 12% Deal Gap. Write which of the Four Paths you would try first — and why. (There is no single right answer; there is only a first move you can explain.)

## Chapter 10: Negotiation From Numbers, Not Emotion

### Anchor to Target Buy

Weak opener: “Would you take less?”  
Stronger frame: “Based on rent and expenses we can defend, we need to be at about $X to make this work as a rental.”

You are not insulting their kitchen. You are describing your constraint.

### Talk about the gap without contempt

- Do share: comps, insurance quotes, repair estimates, rent evidence
- Do not share: “Your house is overpriced and you should feel bad”

Sellers care about net proceeds, timing, certainty, and dignity. Structure paths often win because they solve *their* problem while protecting *your* Target Buy.

### When to walk

If every path to close the gap requires you to violate Target Buy, you walk.

Walking is how you keep the powder dry for the address that fits.

**Try it on a real address:** Write a three-sentence offer frame: (1) respect the property, (2) state your Target Buy logic in plain English, (3) invite a path — price or structure.

## Chapter 11: Your Repeatable Analysis Routine

Run this weekly until it is muscle memory.

1. **Collect** address + asking price  
2. **Establish** defendable rent and expenses (unknowns marked clearly)  
3. **Compute** Income Value → Target Buy → Deal Gap  
4. **Rank** strategies that fit your constraints  
5. **Choose** path(s) to close the gap — or pass  
6. **Log** the decision (address, gap band, pursue/pass, why)

Logging builds pattern recognition faster than random YouTube. After twenty logged addresses you will feel Deal Gap bands in your gut — and you will trust your gut *because* it was trained on written numbers, not vibes.

### Sample log lines

```
123 Oak | Ask 389k | TB 355k | Gap 8.7% | LTR>HH | Path: price + insurance quote | PURSUE
88 Pine | Ask 512k | TB 410k | Gap 20% | LTR fail, WH maybe | Need seller ≤445k | WATCH
41 Elm | Ask 275k | TB 268k | Gap 2.5% | HH | Path: as-is if inspection clean | PURSUE
```

Short logs beat essays. Essays do not get written. Logs do.

### Weekly cadence (suggested)

| Day | Action |
|---|---|
| Mon–Tue | Collect 5–10 new addresses (listings or off-market leads) |
| Wed | Run three-number analysis; discard obvious 15%+ with no path |
| Thu | Deepen top 1–2 (inspection items, insurance quotes, comps) |
| Fri | Offers / conversations / passes — then log |

# Part V — Mistakes, Mindset, and Next Steps

## Chapter 12: The Mistakes That Create Phantom Deals

Watch for these:

1. **Fabricated rent** — “It’ll rent for…” with no comps  
2. **Ignored insurance, taxes, vacancy** — especially in insurance-shock markets  
3. **List price as value** — asking is a wish; Target Buy is a constraint  
4. **Analysis paralysis** — infinite tabs, zero decisions  
5. **Reckless FOMO** — skipping the three numbers because “someone else will get it”  
6. **Spreadsheet theater** — beautiful model, garbage inputs  
7. **Strategy cosplay** — calling it a BRRRR to excuse an LTR that does not work  
8. **Repair amnesia** — underwriting the after-repair rent on the before-repair price without a rehab line  

Phantom deals feel productive. They are expensive hobbies.

### A quick anti-phantom checklist

Before you call anything a “deal,” answer yes to all four:

- [ ] Rent is defendable with comps or honest multi-source estimates  
- [ ] Insurance and taxes are quotes/assessments — not vibes  
- [ ] Target Buy is written and includes your buy discount  
- [ ] You can name a path to close the Deal Gap — or you are passing  

Three yeses and a shrug is not a deal. It is a story.

**Try it on a real address:** Audit an old analysis (or a viral “deal” screenshot). Find one fabricated or missing input. Rewrite the Deal Gap with that input corrected or marked unknown.

## Chapter 13: Confidence Without Expertise

You do not need to predict the market.

You need a **personal buy number** — Target Buy — and the honesty to protect it.

Confidence comes from:

- Transparent assumptions you can explain
- Multi-source data when available (and “Unavailable” when not)
- A routine you trust more than a hype reel

Hours of analysis reduce to three numbers. Expertise is knowing what those numbers mean — and when to walk.

**Try it on a real address:** Explain your Target Buy for one property out loud in 30 seconds. If you cannot, your assumptions are still fog.

## Chapter 14: Put the Method to Work

### One-page recap

1. **Income Value** — ceiling where income stops supporting the deal  
2. **Target Buy** — Income Value after *your* required margin  
3. **Deal Gap** — `(Ask − Target Buy) / Ask × 100`

6. **Walk** when Target Buy cannot be protected

# Appendix — Three Worked Examples

Round numbers for teaching. Full worksheets live in `worked-examples.md` in this package.

## Example A — LTR with a real gap

- Ask **$400,000** · Rent **$2,400/mo** · Vacancy 6% · Normal taxes/insurance/management  
- At asking, cash flow is negative under 25% down / 7% / 30-year financing  
- Teaching result: Income Value **$360,000** → Target Buy (5% off) **$342,000**  
- Deal Gap ≈ **14.5%** (treat as ~15% band)  
- First moves: re-verify rent/insurance, then structure — or pass if seller is firm retail  

**Lesson:** The “1% rule” would have demanded $4,000 rent. Reality was $2,400. Shortcuts create phantom deals.

## Example B — Same house as a house hack

- Same ask **$400,000**  
- Owner occupies; two rooms rent for **$1,750** combined; owner also saves ~**$1,400/mo** vs renting elsewhere  
- Teaching result: Target Buy rises to ~**$383,000** · Deal Gap ~**4%** (0–5% band)  

**Lesson:** Strategy changes Target Buy. The roof did not get better — the definition of “works for me” did.

## Example C — Fixer: LTR pass vs wholesale maybe

- Ask **$275,000** + **$40,000** rehab = **$315,000** all-in  
- Stabilized LTR Target Buy ~**$252,000** → ~**20%** gap on all-in basis → LTR **pass**  
- Wholesale only if you can contract near **$230,000** and an end buyer’s Target Buy clears ~**$245,000** with room for your fee  

**Lesson:** Always underwrite all-in cost. Wholesale is a spread between two Target Buys — not a personality type.

## Glossary (plain English)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Income Value** | Max purchase price before the income property stops working under your assumptions (cash-flow boundary) |
| **Target Buy** | Price that hits your return target — typically Income Value after your buy discount |
| **Deal Gap** | Percent distance from asking price to Target Buy |
| **NOI** | Net operating income — effective income after operating expenses (before debt service) |
| **CoC** | Cash-on-cash return — annual cash flow ÷ cash invested |
| **LTV** | Loan-to-value — loan size as a percent of value/price |
| **Vacancy** | Allowance for empty periods or credit loss |
| **IQ Estimate** | Multi-source blended estimate used in DealGapIQ when sources are available; never fabricated |
| **Buy discount** | Margin you require below Income Value to set Target Buy |
| **Four Paths** | Price, capital, income re-verify, structure — ways to close a Deal Gap |
